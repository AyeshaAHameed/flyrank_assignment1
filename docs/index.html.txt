    <h1>Structured Content Archetype Clustering</h1>
    <p class="abstract">
      We asked whether ~30,000 FlyRank content pages group into a small number of behaviorally
      distinct performance archetypes, without any predefined labels. Using KMeans clustering on
      validated, leakage-free structural and behavioral signals, we found six stable groupings —
      from a clear high-performing "champion" cluster to a clearly underperforming, stale cluster —
      with the majority of pages sitting in an undifferentiated middle. Each archetype maps to one
      of six editorial actions, giving a content strategist a way to prioritize review across a
      large inventory by group rather than by individual page.
    </p>

    <section id="introduction">
      <div class="section-num">01</div>
      <h2>Introduction</h2>
      <p>
        A content strategist managing thousands of pages cannot review each one individually.
        This work supports one decision: which broad action — <strong>protect, improve, rewrite,
        merge, prune, or monitor</strong> — should apply to a group of similarly-behaving pages,
        so review effort can be prioritized by archetype rather than spent page-by-page.
      </p>
      <p>
        A wrong call here has real cost at scale: rewriting pages that would have recovered on
        their own, or folding a genuinely at-risk group into "leave alone" and never revisiting it.
        With no predefined categories to sort pages into, this is an unsupervised problem —
        clustering, not classification.
      </p>
    </section>

    <section id="data">
      <div class="section-num">02</div>
      <h2>Data</h2>
      <p>
        <strong>Release:</strong> the anonymized FlyRank internship warehouse slice,
        <code>content_refresh_anonymized.csv</code> — approximately 30,000 pages, one snapshot,
        44 columns, public-safe (no client names, domains, URLs, or private queries).
      </p>
      <p><strong>Features used:</strong> structural and behavioral signals only —
        <code>word_count</code>, <code>char_count</code>, <code>ctr</code>,
        <code>avg_position</code>, <code>engagement_rate</code>, <code>scroll_rate</code>,
        <code>ai_traffic_pct</code>, <code>days_since_last_update</code>, plus one-hot encoded
        <code>content_type</code> and <code>main_intent</code>.
      </p>
      <div class="callout">
        <strong>Deliberately excluded:</strong> <code>trend_direction</code> and
        <code>trend_pct</code> — outcome-adjacent, label-derived fields that would leak the very
        pattern the model is meant to discover. They are used only afterward, to describe a
        cluster once formed, never to build it. Pseudonymous IDs are used for row identity only,
        never as model inputs.
      </div>
    </section>

    <section id="methodology">
      <div class="section-num">03</div>
      <h2>Methodology</h2>
      <p>
        <strong>Method:</strong> KMeans clustering, chosen because this is an unsupervised
        problem — there is no existing archetype label to predict, only behavior to group by
        similarity.
      </p>
      <h3>Choosing k</h3>
      <p>
        Silhouette score peaked at k=2 (0.533), but that split (2,106 vs. 27,894 pages) collapsed
        to a trivial, uninformative binary division. From k=3 onward, score rose gradually to a
        shallow peak near k=7 (0.333), with no sharp elbow. <strong>k=6</strong> was chosen to
        align cluster count directly with the six-action taxonomy, trading a marginal (~0.004)
        silhouette gain at k=7 for interpretability.
      </p>
      <h3>Baseline</h3>
      <p>
        A transparent, hand-weighted rule from prior work combines visibility (0.45),
        tier-normalized CTR gap (0.40), and staleness (0.15) into a reason code and action label
        per page — using the same underlying signals the clustering model sees, but combined by a
        fixed formula rather than discovered from the data.
      </p>
      <h3>Validation design</h3>
      <p>
        No train/test split applies in the supervised sense. Cluster count was chosen via
        elbow + silhouette across k=2–8; stability was checked by refitting k=6 with three
        different random seeds and comparing assignments via Adjusted Rand Index (ARI).
      </p>
    </section>

    <section id="interpretation">
      <div class="section-num">05</div>
      <h2>Interpretation</h2>
      <p>
        The clustering found a clear "best" archetype (Cluster 1) and a clear "worst" archetype
        (Cluster 0), separated mainly by search position and staleness — consistent with the
        CTR-vs-position relationship independently validated in earlier baseline work. The
        majority of pages (Clusters 2 and 3, ~24,700 pages combined) sit in an undifferentiated
        middle with no strong signal in either direction.
      </p>
      <p>
        <strong>This is an honest finding, not a modeling shortfall:</strong> most content is
        neither excellent nor urgently broken. Clusters 2 and 3 were only weakly distinguished
        from each other — the model did not surface as much fine-grained structure within the
        "average" majority as initially hoped, and that negative result is reported as-is rather
        than forced into an artificially sharper story.
      </p>
    </section>

    <section id="limitations">
      <div class="section-num">06</div>
      <h2>Limitations</h2>
      <ul>
        <li>Single snapshot — archetype stability over time is unverified with this release.</li>
        <li>No ground-truth labels — validation is internal (silhouette, seed stability), not accuracy against a known-correct grouping.</li>
        <li>ARI of 0.747 between two of three seeds means some pages have soft, not fixed, cluster membership.</li>
        <li>Cluster 4's CTR values are very likely a data artifact and should not be read as a real high-performing archetype without further investigation.</li>
        <li>Clusters 2 and 3 are only weakly separated from each other.</li>
        <li>All findings are <strong>observed, directional, and decision-support</strong> — this work makes no causal claims and does not model or predict Google's ranking algorithm.</li>
        <li>Based on an anonymized sample; client- or topic-specific effects may not generalize.</li>
      </ul>
    </section>
